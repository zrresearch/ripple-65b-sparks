Ripple on Four Sparks

A four-Spark serving recipe for a 65B dense model with a 1M-token context, built from a two-Spark one.

zrresearch Nov 10, 2026

Ricursive's Ripple-65B Preview is a 65-billion-parameter dense transformer with a 1M-token context, released on November 4 under Apache-2.0. Its card reports 80.5 on Terminal-Bench 2.1, level with open models ten times its size. Unlike the mixture-of-experts models we usually serve, it reads every weight for every token, and on a DGX Spark, where decode is limited by memory bandwidth, that makes it slow: about 4 to 5 tokens per second on one GB10. Spreading the model over more Sparks divides the weights each one reads per token. Within a week of release, community work in TensorFold served Ripple on one GB10 and on two, the second with the full 1M context, but nothing ran it on four.

We built that: a recipe that serves Ripple across four Sparks on a QSFP switch with the full context and Ricursive's speculative-decoding draft model, starting from the community two-Spark recipe. Extending the engine from two ranks to four took 21 lines and gave byte-identical replies, but it read prompts only 1.38 times as fast as two Sparks, and many streams at once decoded slower than four. Five changes to the engine fixed both, and they apply to two Sparks as well.

On four Sparks, one stream decodes code at 17.6 tokens per second, or 57 with the draft model, 1.7 and 1.6 times two Sparks. Prompts are read at about 1,100 tokens per second, twice the first four-Spark build, and a 1M-token prompt in 47 minutes against 1 hour 48 minutes on two Sparks. Sixteen streams decode 229 tokens per second in total on code and 189 on prose. On our test suite, the engine changes leave quality unchanged, and the build scores 1.6 to 5.4 points below the card on the three tests the card reports, mostly because some replies run past our 32,768-token limit without answering. Data, logs, patches and scripts are in the research repository.

Speed on two and four Sparks

Each Spark holds one slice of every layer and the Sparks exchange partial results over their ConnectX-7 fabric in every layer: a direct cable between the two-Spark pair, a QSFP switch for the four. Every build here serves the same 5.0-bit EXL3 pack. We compare three of them: the published two-Spark recipe, the first four-Spark build, which is that engine admitted to four ranks and nothing else, and the final build, which adds the five engine changes described in section 4, on two Sparks and on four. Prompt reading is one cold prompt at a time; decode uses SparkMan's benchmark with the draft model on.

Reading prompts

Before the engine changes, four Sparks read prompts only 1.38 times as fast as two, about 570 tokens per second. The final build roughly doubles that at every length up to 64K, and two Sparks with the same changes read 46 to 53% faster than before, faster than the first four-Spark build. The gain shrinks at very long prompts, where attention over the whole context takes a larger share: the 1M-token retrieval prompt reads at 357 tokens per second on four Sparks and 187 on two, and on both the model returns the exact code hidden 60% of the way in.

Decoding many streams

The first four-Spark build stalled under load: eight code streams decoded 114 tokens per second in total, less than four. A round shared by several streams held at most 16 rows, so eight streams could verify one draft each at most, and sixteen none. The final build lets a shared round take up to 64 rows and prices each round from measured costs. Code now scales with streams, to 198 tokens per second at eight and 229 at sixteen, past our target of 200. Prose gains less, because sampled text is harder to draft: at sixteen streams the engine mostly runs plain rounds, which are faster there than drafting, and reaches 189.

At one stream the changes make no difference; the small differences in the first row of Table 3 are within run-to-run spread. One plain stream decodes at 17 to 19 tokens per second on four Sparks, at the top of the 15 to 20 we estimated from memory bandwidth and 1.7 times two Sparks. The gain is short of 2 because every layer still exchanges partial results across the switch, and that cost does not shrink as ranks are added. With the draft model the gain is smaller, 1.5 to 1.6 times, since a drafted round is shorter and the exchanges are a larger part of it. How much the draft model helps depends on the text: three times on code, one and a half on chat.

Context

Ripple's cache is small for its size, about 32 KB per token in FP8, because only 16 of its 80 layers attend over the whole context. Each Spark holds 26.9 GiB of weights and 16.1 GiB of cache for a 1M-token window, and still has about 80 GiB free. The cache is not pooled across streams, though: each stream reserves its full window, so the recipe serves one stream of 1M tokens or sixteen of 128K, not sixteen of 1M, which was our original target.

Quality

A serving recipe is only useful if the model still answers well through it. We ran the four-Spark build through the fixed test suite we use for every model. It runs the tests with Inspect against the served model: the same items and prompts every time, temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, and at most 32,768 output tokens per reply. The card gives no sampling advice, so these are the suite's defaults. The server ran eight streams of 256K tokens with the draft model on.

On the three tests the card reports, the four-Spark build scores 5.4, 3.3 and 1.6 points lower. AIME, the test with the longest reasoning per item, is within noise of the card. The gaps on GPQA and MMLU-Pro are larger than their standard errors, but most of them come from one place: replies that never finished. Twenty GPQA replies ran to the 32,768-token limit, and none of them reached an answer. Some repeat the same few lines; others are still weighing the options when they stop. Among the replies that finished, GPQA scores 88.8, above the card's figure.

That does not show the 5-bit pack matches the full-precision model. Scoring only finished replies favours us, the card does not say how long it let the model think, and our prompts are not Ricursive's. What it does show is that, at this limit, a few percent of Ripple's replies run away, and that this costs more than any measured difference in quantization.

The long-context test is the clearest case. Every retrieval item passed, 48 of 48, at both 32K and 128K. Every miss was a reply cut off at the limit: three of sixteen variable-tracking items, and all sixteen common-word items. That task asks for the ten most frequent words in a list of about 5,800, and Ripple sets out to count them by hand, copying the list a word at a time, which cannot finish in 32,768 tokens.

No test changes by more than chance. The final build rounds some arithmetic differently, and at temperature 0.6 that sends long replies down different paths, so items flip in both directions, and they flip about equally. AIME is the closest case. With four distinct samples per problem the final build scores 90.8 ± 4.9, and the misses fall on three problems: two it solves in two and three of four tries, and one, problem 14, whose solution took the first build 29,294 tokens and which ran past the 32,768-token limit in all four tries on the final build. One sample per problem on the first build cannot tell a coin flip from a change. The long-context test gained the most time, as expected from faster prompt reading. BFCL multi-turn, made of many short turns, was the only test not faster.

The rerun also exposed two faults in our suite, which affected earlier runs as well. MMLU-Pro's own loader shuffled its 12,032 questions without a seed before our seeded sample took 700, so each run drew a different 700: the first rerun shared only 41 questions with the baseline and scored 85.0 on its own set. And the suite sent the same seed with every request, so AIME's four samples of each problem were identical, which is why Table 5 counts 30 replies rather than 120. The suite's next version lists the 700 MMLU-Pro questions the first run drew and sends AIME no seed, and changes nothing else; Table 6's MMLU-Pro and AIME rows come from it.

Building the recipe

The model

Ripple has 80 layers with a hidden size of 5,120, 80 query heads and 8 key-value heads of 128 dimensions, a 40,960-wide feed-forward block and a 100,352-token vocabulary. Every fifth layer attends over the whole context without positional encoding; the other 64 use rotary positions and a 1,024-token sliding window. Each attention output passes through a sigmoid gate, each layer carries a residual connection on its values, and the feed-forward activations are clipped.

Two properties decide how it serves. Because only 16 layers keep the full context, a 1M-token context needs tens of gigabytes of cache rather than hundreds. And because the model is dense, a decode step reads all of its weights: 39 GB at 5 bits per weight, which a single GB10 at 273 GB/s of memory bandwidth can stream at most about seven times a second. Splitting the weights over two Sparks halves that read, and over four quarters it, at the cost of an exchange between the Sparks in every layer. The model divides cleanly four ways: 20 query heads, 2 key-value heads, a quarter of the feed-forward block and a quarter of the vocabulary per Spark.

The two-Spark path was the starting point. Reproducing two Sparks

We built the two-Spark engine from the recipe at its pinned commit; its source tree hashes to the value the recipe records. We downloaded the BF16 release, the DFlash2 draft model and the published 5.0-bit pack at pinned revisions; the weights at our revision are identical to those the published pack was made from. From the BF16 weights we also made our own 5.0-bit pack on one Spark, with the same ExLlamaV3 commit and settings, in 5 hours 39 minutes. It matches the published one in everything but the quantized values: shard sizes, index, quantization settings, per-tensor bit allocation, config and tokenizer are byte-identical.

From two ranks to four

Most of the two-Spark engine was already written for any number of ranks. The weights, attention heads, feed-forward block, vocabulary and draft model are all cut by the rank count, and rank 0 sends each call's plan to every other rank, which all run the same follower loop. What held it to two was a single admission check, and a fast path for small gathers built for exactly one peer.

At four ranks every cut lands on whole 128-column blocks, as the EXL3 kernels require: 20 of the 80 query heads per rank, 2 of the 8 key-value heads, 10,240 of the 40,960 feed-forward columns and 25,088 of the 100,352 vocabulary rows. Three ranks cannot work, since 80 heads do not split three ways, so the engine now admits one, two or four ranks. For the small gathers at four ranks it borrows TensorFold's multirank RoCE path, the one GLM uses at three or more ranks. Each rank first checks one gather over RoCE against NCCL, byte for byte, and the RoCE path is used only if every rank agrees. The change is 21 lines in the engine plus a 311-line copy of GLM's RoCE module renamed for Ripple, and a launcher that loops over its workers. At two ranks it gives the original build's bytes (Table 7).

Making four Sparks fast

On the switch

The four Sparks are joined by a QSFP switch, one link each. The first four-Spark build ran there with the engine and launcher unchanged apart from the switch's RoCE settings: the IPv4 RoCE v2 address sits at GID index 5 there, against 3 on the direct-cabled pair, and one Spark names its ports differently from the others, so the launcher takes a list of RoCE devices per rank. The launcher also needed one fix: a Spark that had never served Ripple had no folder for its GPU lock and read as busy. A cold start takes 20 to 60 seconds once the weights are in the page cache.

Where prompt time went

A profile of rank 0 reading a 7,647-token prompt showed where the time went: 57% in the EXL3 matrix multiplies and 32% in NCCL gathers, which sent every rank the full fp32 partial results of every layer. The EXL3 prompt path decodes each 128-row block of weights again for every block of prompt rows, which held it near 30 TFLOPS. After the first two changes, the prompt projections run at about 68 TFLOPS and each rank receives only its share of the sums, but the exchanges still take 28% of the time, so they are the next limit. For decode the cause was a limit rather than a cost, the 16-row cap on shared rounds.

The two-Spark recipe takes the same changes. Prompt reading gains about half there, less than on four Sparks because the reduce-scatter only applies at three ranks or more, and one-stream decode is unchanged.

Limitations

The cache is not pooled. Each stream reserves its whole window, so the recipe serves one 1M-token stream or sixteen of 128K, not sixteen long ones at once.

Replies that run away. At a 32,768-token limit, 1 to 10% of replies on the reasoning tests never finish, and they decide most of the gap to the card. We have not run the suite with a longer limit, and the card does not say what limit it used.

One reply per item. At temperature 0.6 replies vary from run to run. The suite answers each item once, apart from AIME, so a single test can move a point or two by chance; the item-by-item comparison in Table 6 is the guard against reading too much into that.

The draft model at temperature 0.6. At greedy decoding the draft model does not change replies (Table 7). With sampling we have not tested whether it does.

One conversion of our own pack. Our pack is slightly further from BF16 than the reference pack. A second conversion would show whether that is run-to-run variation; we used the reference pack throughout.

One fabric each. The two-Spark pair uses a direct cable and the four Sparks a switch, so the gains from two to four mix the effect of more Sparks with the effect of the network.

Speed runs. Most speed figures are a single run or a median of three. Differences of a few percent are within run-to-run spread, as the four-stream prose figure shows.

What we learned

Four Sparks made one stream 1.7 times faster than two. Each added Spark cuts the weights each one reads per token. The exchange between Sparks in every layer does not shrink, so the gain is short of 2, and smaller still with the draft model.

Most of the speed was in the software. The first four-Spark build read prompts only 38% faster than two Sparks and decoded eight streams slower than four. Neither was a hardware limit: one was a prompt path that re-decoded its weights for every block of rows and an exchange that sent every rank every other rank's full partial results, the other a 16-row cap and a pricing rule that charged the wrong rounds. With the same changes, two Sparks now read prompts faster than four did before.

Rerunning the suite is worth it. Rerunning our test suite on the final build showed that the engine changes left quality alone, and it also found two faults in the suite itself that a single run could not show. Every model we test from now on gets the fixed version.

Appendix: reproducing this work

The recipe is ajensenwaud's two-Spark recipe with four patches from the research repository applied by git am, in order: four-rank support and the list of workers; per-rank RoCE device names; a fix for a Spark's first start; and the five engine changes. Its build script then applies the TensorFold patches tensorfold-0010 to 0015 to TensorFold v1.2.3 and checks the result against the pinned source tree before building. On a switch, set the RoCE GID index the switch uses (5 for us) and each rank's devices if a Spark names them differently.

Each speed figure has its script and raw output under benchmarks/, with every Phase 4 run recorded in benchmarks/phase4/RESULTS.md. The quality suite and its per-item results are in zrresearch/evals, under the labels ripple-65b-4x (first build), ripple-65b-4x-p4 and ripple-65b-4x-p4-s2 (final build).
