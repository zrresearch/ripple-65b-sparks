Ripple on Four Sparks

Building a four-Spark serving recipe for a 65B dense model, starting from a two-Spark one.

zrresearch Nov 10, 2026

Ricursive's Ripple-65B Preview is a 65-billion-parameter dense transformer with a 1M-token context, released on November 4 under Apache-2.0. Its card reports 80.5 on Terminal-Bench 2.1, level with open models ten times its size. Unlike the mixture-of-experts models we usually serve, it reads every weight for every token, and on a DGX Spark, where decode is limited by memory bandwidth, that makes it slow: about 4 to 5 tokens per second on one GB10.

Spreading the model over more Sparks divides the weights each one reads per token. Within a week of release, community work in TensorFold served Ripple on one GB10 and on two, the second with the full 1M context, but no serving stack runs it on four. We want to build that: a recipe that serves Ripple across four Sparks on a QSFP switch, with the full context, sixteen concurrent streams and the publisher's speculative-decoding draft model, measured the way we measured apex-flash-1.

This page records the work as it happens. It starts with what already exists and our plan; results replace the plan as they come in. Phases 1 to 3 are done: the two-Spark recipe reproduces, the engine runs on two or four ranks, and on four Sparks Ripple gives byte-identical replies to the two-Spark build at 1.5 to 1.75 times its speed. We then ran our standard test suite against the four-Spark build. It scores 1.6 to 5.4 points below the card on the tests the card reports, and most of that gap is replies that ran past our 32,768-token limit without answering.

Phase 4 made the four-Spark build faster where it was slow. Five engine changes double prompt reading, to about 1,100 tokens per second, and lift sixteen concurrent streams to 229 tokens per second on code and 189 on prose, past our target of 200 on code. A 1M-token prompt now reads in 47 minutes, against 1 hour 48 minutes on two Sparks. One-stream speed is unchanged.

The model

Ripple has 80 layers with a hidden size of 5,120, 80 query heads and 8 key-value heads of 128 dimensions, a 40,960-wide feed-forward block and a 100,352-token vocabulary. Every fifth layer attends over the whole context without positional encoding; the other 64 use rotary positions and a 1,024-token sliding window. Each attention output passes through a sigmoid gate, each layer carries a residual connection on its values, and the feed-forward activations are clipped.

Two properties decide how it serves. Because only 16 layers keep the full context, the cache costs about 32 KB per token in FP8 or 64 KB in BF16, so a 1M-token context needs tens of gigabytes rather than hundreds. And because the model is dense, a decode step reads all of its weights: 39 GB at 5 bits per weight, which a single GB10 at 273 GB/s of memory bandwidth can stream at most about seven times a second. Splitting the weights over two Sparks halves that read, and over four quarters it, at the cost of an exchange between the Sparks in every layer.

The model divides cleanly four ways. Each rank would hold 20 query heads, 2 key-value heads, a quarter of the feed-forward block and a quarter of the vocabulary.

The two-Spark path is the starting point. It runs from EXL3 packs made with a branch of ExLlamaV3 that adds Ripple's value residual, holds 19.8 GiB of weights per Spark at 5 bits, and passed a retrieval test at 1,018,419 tokens using 52.7 GiB per Spark. It exchanges partial results over NCCL, with small gathers sent as direct RoCE writes. It accepts exactly two ranks and refuses any other count by name.

Two of its numbers frame the goal. A cold 1M-token prompt is read at about 156 tokens per second, roughly 1.8 hours, so a full window is practical only when it builds up over a conversation and stays cached. And the cache is not pooled: 1M tokens of context fits at one stream, 256K at four.

On four Sparks we expect roughly 15 to 20 tokens per second for one plain stream, each Spark reading about 10 GB of weights per token, and 40 or more with the draft model. Our target for the recipe is the full 1M context, sixteen concurrent streams, and 200 tokens per second across them. The last figure is a stretch, and we will report what we measure against it.

Phase 1: reproducing two Sparks

We built the two-Spark engine from the recipe at its pinned commit; its source tree hashes to the value the recipe records. We downloaded the BF16 release, the DFlash2 draft model and the published 5.0-bit pack at pinned revisions. The weights at our revision are identical to those the published pack was made from; later commits changed only code and documentation. From the BF16 weights we made our own 5.0-bit pack on one Spark, with the same ExLlamaV3 commit and settings. It took 5 hours 39 minutes.

Our pack matches the published one in everything but the quantized values. The shard sizes, the index, the quantization settings, the per-tensor bit allocation, the config and the tokenizer are byte-identical; the five weight shards are not, as with any re-run of a GPU quantizer.

The reference pack reproduces the published perplexity to three decimals. Our pack is slightly but consistently further from BF16: 0.25% higher perplexity, and a mean KL divergence about a third higher, in every band of the reference model's confidence but the most certain. Both packs came from the same quantizer commit, settings and source weights, so the likeliest cause is variation between conversion runs. A second conversion would measure that spread; until then we continue from the reference pack, which is the better of the two.

The long context reproduces as well. We built a 1,015,070-token prompt from WikiText-2 articles, hid one sentence giving an access code 60% of the way in, and asked for the code at the end. The two Sparks read the prompt at 156.6 tokens per second, in 1 hour 48 minutes, against the published 156; the model returned the exact code and then decoded at 5.2 tokens per second with the draft model on. The two-Spark recipe does what it reports, and Phase 2 starts from it with the reference pack.

Phase 2: two ranks to four

Most of the two-Spark engine was already written for any number of ranks. The weights, the attention heads, the feed-forward block, the vocabulary and the draft model are all cut by the rank count, and rank 0 sends each call's plan to every other rank, which all run the same follower loop. What held it to two was a single admission check, and a fast path for small gathers built for exactly one peer.

At four ranks every cut lands on whole 128-column blocks, as the EXL3 kernels require: 20 of the 80 query heads per rank, 2 of the 8 key-value heads, 10,240 of the 40,960 feed-forward columns and 25,088 of the 100,352 vocabulary rows. The draft model divides the same way. Three ranks cannot work, since 80 heads do not split three ways, so the engine now admits one, two or four ranks and refuses any other count by name.

For the small gathers at four ranks, the engine borrows TensorFold's multirank RoCE path, the one GLM uses at three or more ranks. Each rank first checks one gather over RoCE against NCCL, byte for byte, and the RoCE path is used only if every rank agrees; otherwise NCCL carries everything. TensorFold measured that path at 1 to 2.5% faster decode on three GB10s, so it is an option, not a requirement. The launcher takes a list of workers in rank order, and with one worker it behaves as before.

Phase 3: four Sparks

The four-Spark group is joined by a QSFP switch, one link per Spark. We served the reference pack on it with the Phase 2 engine and launcher unchanged, apart from the switch's RoCE settings: the IPv4 RoCE v2 address sits at GID index 5 there, against 3 on the direct-cabled pair, and one Spark names its two ports differently from the others, so the launcher now takes a port list per rank. The launcher also needed one fix: a Spark that had never served Ripple had no folder for its GPU lock, and read as busy.

Each rank holds 26.9 GiB of weights and 16.1 GiB of cache for a 1M-token window, half the 32.1 GiB a rank needs on two Sparks, and still has about 80 GiB free. The small gathers ran over the multirank RoCE path on all four ranks, after each checked its first gather against NCCL byte for byte. A cold start takes 20 to 60 seconds once the weights are in the page cache.

One plain stream decodes at 17 to 19 tokens per second, at the top of our 15 to 20 estimate and 1.7 times the two-Spark speed. Phase 4: speed

Phase 3 left two weak spots. Prompt reading scaled poorly: four Sparks read a prompt only 1.38 times as fast as two, about 570 tokens per second. And many streams stalled: eight code streams decoded at 115 tokens per second in total, less than four. We profiled both, changed the engine five times, and measured each change against the build before it with the same binary where we could. Every number in this section comes from the recipe rebuilt from its own scripts with all five changes.

A profile of rank 0 reading a 7,647-token prompt showed where the time went: 57% in the EXL3 matrix multiplies and 32% in NCCL gathers, which sent every rank the full fp32 partial results of every layer. The EXL3 prompt path decodes each 128-row block of weights again for every block of prompt rows, which held it near 30 TFLOPS. For decode, the cause was a limit, not a cost: a round shared by several streams held at most 16 rows across all of them, so eight streams could verify one draft each at most and sixteen none.

Our target was sixteen streams with the full 1M context. The cache is still not pooled across streams, so the recipe serves one stream of 1M tokens or sixteen of 128K, not both at once. The test suite, rerun on the Phase 4 build, finds no change in quality (Table 13).

Quality: our standard suite

A serving recipe is only useful if the model still answers well through it, so we ran the four-Spark build through the fixed test suite we now use for every model (version 2026.11-1). It runs the tests with Inspect against the served model: the same items, prompts and seed every time (MMLU-Pro aside; see below), one sample per item, temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, and at most 32,768 output tokens per reply. The card gives no sampling advice, so these are the suite's defaults. The server ran eight streams of 256K tokens with the draft model on. The eight tests below took 26 hours 24 minutes.

On the three tests the card reports, the four-Spark build scores 5.4, 3.3 and 1.6 points lower. AIME, the test with the longest reasoning per item, is within noise of the card. The gaps on GPQA and MMLU-Pro are larger than their standard errors, but most of them come from one place: replies that never finished. Twenty GPQA replies ran to the 32,768-token limit, and none of them reached an answer. Some repeat the same few lines; others are still weighing the options when they stop. Among the replies that finished, GPQA scores 88.8, above the card's figure.

That does not show the 5-bit pack matches the full-precision model. Scoring only finished replies favours us, the card does not say how long it let the model think, and our prompts are not Ricursive's. What it does show is that, at this limit, a few percent of Ripple's replies run away, and that this costs more than any measured difference in quantization. At greedy decoding the draft model does not change the output (Table 5); at temperature 0.6 we have not tested that.

The long-context test is the clearest case. Every retrieval item passed, 48 of 48, at both 32K and 128K. Every miss was a reply cut off at the limit: three of sixteen variable-tracking items, and all sixteen common-word items. That task asks for the ten most frequent words in a list of about 5,800, and Ripple sets out to count them by hand, copying the list a word at a time, which cannot finish in 32,768 tokens. Reading the prompts was slow as well. With eight streams reading 119,000-token prompts at once, each waited about 30 to 35 minutes for its first token, which is what Phase 4's prompt-reading work has to improve.

The Phase 4 build ran the same suite again, on the same four Sparks with the same server settings. It took 18 hours 20 minutes instead of 26 hours 24 minutes.

No test changes by more than chance. The Phase 4 build rounds some arithmetic differently, and at temperature 0.6 that sends long replies down different paths, so items flip in both directions, and they flip about equally. The two newly missed AIME problems both ran to the 32,768-token limit on the Phase 4 build, where the Phase 3 build had finished them in 29,294 and 20,378 tokens; nothing was answered wrongly. The long-context test gained the most time, as expected from Phase 4's prompt reading. BFCL multi-turn, made of many short turns, was the only test not faster.

The rerun also showed two faults in our suite, which affect the earlier runs as well. MMLU-Pro's own loader shuffles its 12,032 questions without a seed before our seeded sample takes 700, so each run draws a different 700; the two runs share only 41, and their headline scores, 80.9 and 85.0, are not comparable. And the suite sends the same seed with every request, so AIME's four samples of each problem were identical: the test measured 30 replies, not 120, and Table 8's standard error for it is the one for 30. We will fix both in the suite's next version, and rerun those two tests on the Phase 4 build.

The two agent tests in the suite, Terminal-Bench 2 through its reference agent and Cybench through Codex, run next on the same build. Their results will be added here.
